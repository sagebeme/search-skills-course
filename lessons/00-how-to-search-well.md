# Lesson 00: How to Search Well

**Goal:** learn the habits that make every search better, on any website, and
learn a quick way to check whether what you found is true.

**Time:** about 30 minutes.

---

## 1. A search engine matches words, not ideas

When you type into Google, it looks for pages that contain words like the ones
you typed. It doesn't really understand what you *mean*. So the words you pick
matter more than anything else.

**Bad:** `how do I get better at computers`
**Better:** `beginner free course learn python programming`

The second one uses the words a helpful page would actually *contain*.

### The trick: think like the page you want

Ask yourself: *"If the perfect page existed, what words would be written on it?"*
Then search for those words.

- Want a free textbook? The page probably says `"open textbook"` or `pdf` or
  `free download` and has the subject's name.
- Want official numbers? The page is probably from a government office and says
  `statistics` or `report` with a year.

## 2. Start broad, then narrow down

1. Do a quick, simple search.
2. Look at the first results. Notice the **words they use**, especially words
   experts use that you didn't know.
3. Search again using those better words.

Example: you search `why is my phone battery dying fast` and the results keep
mentioning "battery health" and "background apps". Your next search becomes
`check battery health android background apps`. Much better results.

> This is the single most important habit in the whole course. Every search
> teaches you the words for the next one.

## 3. Use fewer, stronger words

Leave out little filler words like "how do I", "the", "please". Keep the words
that carry the meaning.

`what is the best way that I can learn to speak French for free` becomes
`learn French free beginner`.

## 4. Try more than one place

Google is just one tool. Some questions are better answered by:

- **Reddit:** real people's experiences ("is this course worth it?").
- **Wikipedia:** a quick overview, plus a list of sources at the bottom.
- **A specialist site:** research papers, official statistics, documentation.

Lesson 03 covers more of these.

## 5. Checking if information is true: the SIFT method

Finding information is half the job. The other half is not being fooled.
Researcher Mike Caulfield created a simple four-step habit called **SIFT**:

1. **S — Stop.** Before you believe or share something, pause. Do you know this
   website or person? Does the post make you feel very angry or very excited?
   Strong feelings are a warning sign: that's how fake news spreads.
2. **I — Investigate the source.** Who made this? Open a new tab and search for
   the website or author's name, plus words like `review` or `scam` or
   `who owns`. Don't trust what the site says about itself.
3. **F — Find better coverage.** Search for the same claim and see if trusted
   sources (big news sites, official bodies, experts) say the same thing.
4. **T — Trace it to the original.** Quotes, numbers and photos get passed
   around and changed. Follow the links back to where they first came from.

The trick inside SIFT is called **reading sideways** (experts call it "lateral
reading"): instead of reading one page from top to bottom, open **new tabs** to
check what *other* sites say about it. Professional fact-checkers do this
within seconds.

### Quick trust questions for any source

| Question | Good sign | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Who wrote it? | A named person or organisation you can look up | No name, or a name you can't find anywhere else |
| When? | A date, recently updated | No date, or very old for a fast-changing topic |
| Where does it get its facts? | Links to sources you can check | "Studies show..." with no link |
| What does it want from you? | Nothing, or to teach | Money, your phone number, a download, urgent action |
| How does it make you feel? | Calm, informed | Angry, scared, "too good to be true" |

## Exercise

Pick something you actually want to learn this month (a skill, a subject, a
question you've had for ages). Then:

1. Write your question the way you'd normally say it.
2. Rewrite it as 3–6 strong search words, thinking "like the page you want".
3. Search once. Write down **two new words** you saw in the results that you
   didn't know before.
4. Search again using those new words. Was it better? Write one sentence on why.
5. Pick one result and run SIFT on it: who made it, what do other sites say
   about them, and can you trace one fact on the page back to its original
   source?

Keep your answers. You'll use this topic again in the final project.
